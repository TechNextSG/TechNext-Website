What is ERP? A plain-language guide for growing companies

ERP is the software a company runs on: one system, one set of records, for sales, stock, purchasing and money. This guide explains what that means in practice, without the jargon.

In one paragraph

ERP (enterprise resource planning) software runs a company's core processes, such as sales, purchasing, inventory, accounting and HR, on one shared database. Every team works from the same customers, products and accounts, so an order entered once flows through to delivery, invoice and the books without anyone typing it again.

What ERP stands for, and what it actually means

ERP stands for enterprise resource planning. The name is older than the idea it now describes: in practice, an ERP system is the operational backbone of a business. Instead of a CRM for sales, a spreadsheet for stock, a separate tool for purchasing and accounting software for the books, one system holds all of it, and every step updates the same records.

Take a single customer order. In an ERP it moves like this:

  1. Sales confirms a quotation, and it becomes a sales order.
  2. The order reserves stock in the warehouse, or triggers a purchase or production order if stock is short.
  3. The warehouse ships it, and the delivery updates stock levels.
  4. The invoice is created from the order, and the accounting entries post themselves.
  5. When the customer pays, the bank line reconciles against the invoice.
QuotationSales orderDeliveryInvoicePayment & books

Nobody re-keys anything, and anyone can see where the order is. That is the whole point of ERP.

What an ERP system usually covers

  • Finance and accounting: the general ledger, invoicing, payables and receivables, bank reconciliation, tax and financial reports.
  • Sales and CRM: leads, quotations, orders, pricelists and customer history.
  • Inventory and purchasing: stock across warehouses, reordering, vendor bills matched to receipts.
  • Manufacturing: bills of materials, work orders and product costing.
  • HR and payroll: employees, leave, expenses and pay.
  • Projects and services: tasks, timesheets and billing by milestone or time.
  • Point of sale and eCommerce: shops and online stores tied to the same stock and accounts.

Modern ERPs are modular: you switch on the parts you need. With Odoo, which TechNext implements, each of these is an app on the same database, so a company can start with three and add the rest later without an integration project.

ERP versus accounting software

It is a fair question for any company moving off tools like QuickBooks or Xero. Accounting software records transactions after they happen. An ERP runs the operations that create them.

Accounting softwareERP
Main jobKeep the books and file taxRun sales, stock, purchasing and the books together
Where orders liveElsewhere (spreadsheets, a CRM, email)In the same system as the invoice
StockBasic, or not at allReal stock by warehouse, with reordering
Typical userThe finance teamEvery team
Best forSmall, service-only businessesCompanies with stock, multiple teams or several entities

Signs a company needs an ERP

There is no size threshold. The signal is friction: the same data typed into several places, stock numbers nobody trusts, a month-end that takes a week of reconciling, or reports that depend on one person's spreadsheet. We list seven of these in Seven signs your business has outgrown spreadsheets.

Cloud or on-premise, suite or best-of-breed

Two choices shape every ERP decision. The first is where it runs: in the vendor's cloud, on a platform you control, or on your own servers. For Odoo that means Odoo Online, Odoo.sh or on-premise, which we compare in a separate guide. The second is suite or best-of-breed: one system for everything, or separate specialist tools stitched together. Best-of-breed can look cheaper at the start, but every connection between tools is something to build, monitor and fix. A suite avoids that by design.

Where Odoo fits

Common questions

What does ERP stand for?

Enterprise resource planning: software that runs a company's core processes, such as sales, inventory, purchasing and accounting, on one shared database.

Is ERP only for large companies?

No. Modular ERPs such as Odoo let a small company start with a few apps and add more as it grows. The trigger is friction between tools, not headcount.

What is the difference between ERP and CRM?

A CRM manages leads and customer relationships. An ERP covers the whole operation, and usually includes a CRM, so a won deal becomes an order, a delivery and an invoice in the same system.
